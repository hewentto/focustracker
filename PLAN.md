# Building the Best Jared

The system, the programme, and — just as importantly — the list of popular things deliberately **not** in it.

Every number below traces to a study. Where the evidence is weak, it says so. Where the fitness/wellness consensus is wrong, it says that too.

---

## 0. The one thing that decides whether any of this works

You said it yourself: *"I try to go early like 6/7am and if I don't because I stayed up too late it doesn't happen."*

That is the whole system in one sentence. Your training doesn't fail at the gym, it fails the night before. So the architecture is:

```
wake time  →  training happens  →  everything downstream
     ↑
  the only item that is non-negotiable
```

Wake time is the **anchor**. It is the one thing tracked as a hard daily. Everything else is either downstream of it or cheap enough not to matter.

The evidence for anchoring the *wake* end rather than the bedtime: Windred et al. 2024 (UK Biobank, N=60,977) found the Sleep Regularity Index predicted mortality better than sleep duration, and with regularity in the model, duration added nothing significant. Phillips et al. 2017 found irregular sleepers with **identical total sleep time** had melatonin onset ~2.6h later. Your 7.5h average is fine. The variance is the problem.

**Not "wake at 5am."** That claim has no causal support. The GWAS (Jones et al. 2019, N=697,828) found the top 5% and bottom 5% of morningness genotypes differ by **25 minutes** of actual sleep timing. And Yam, Fehr & Barnes (2014) found supervisors rate early-starting employees as more conscientious *independent of actual output* — some of the "early risers perform better" literature is measuring the rater, not the riser. Consistency is the evidenced variable. Earliness is aesthetics.

## 1. Daily core — six one-tap items

| # | Item | Why it's daily |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Woke within ±30 min of target** | The anchor. Sleep Regularity Index (Windred 2024). Gates everything else. |
| 2 | **Outside within 90 min of waking, 10+ min** | The strongest *causal mechanism* in the whole review. Wright et al. 2013: one week of natural light advanced melatonin onset **~2 hours**, within-subject, objective endpoint. Late chronotypes shifted most. Phase-resetting is inherently daily — a two-hour Saturday walk does nothing for Tuesday's phase. |
| 3 | **Trained** (lift / run / stair snacks) | One binary covering all movement. Exercise-type logging is the *only* self-monitoring category that holds up long-term — 61% sustained adherence at 11 months vs 21% for diet logging (JMIR mHealth 2023). |
| 4 | **Caffeine within plan** | Gardiner 2023: pooled −45 min sleep, −11.4 min deep sleep. Feeds straight back into item 1. |
| 5 | **Both binding blocks ran** | WEIS 2017: imposed blocking +22–24% productivity; the same tool self-administered produced nothing. |
| 6 | **Logged today** | Harkin et al. 2016, 138 RCTs, N=19,951: progress monitoring → goal attainment **d+ = 0.40**, and *recorded* beats unrecorded. |

### The bad-day rule

**Items 1–3 are the Core Three.** On a day that's falling apart, hitting those three counts as a win and the day is not a failure.

This isn't softness, it's the data. Lally et al. 2010 measured what a missed day actually costs: **0.29 automaticity points, no significant long-term effect**, and non-consecutive performance still built the habit. Streak-loss mechanics manufacture a failure the evidence says didn't happen. The tracker flags **missed twice running**, never missed once.

### An honest tension

You asked for 10+ items. The adherence literature says three daily binaries.

I've built six, and here's the reasoning rather than a fudge: the constraint that actually predicts abandonment is **logging cost per item, not item count**. Diet logging (many entries, evaluative, high cognitive cost) collapses to 21% sustained; exercise logging (one binary tap) holds at 61%. Six one-tap binaries are cheaper than two multi-field forms.

And the "one habit at a time" rule is *not* supported: the only head-to-head RCT (Vandelanotte et al. 2007, N=567) found **no difference** between simultaneous and sequential adoption, and a meta-analysis of 69 RCTs (N=73,873) found interventions targeting more behaviours showed **no reduced effectiveness**. Behaviours co-move — "coaction" appeared in 70.8% of treatment-group analyses vs 12.5% of controls (N=9,228).

So: six one-tap dailies is defensible. Fourteen is not. Everything else goes weekly.

## 3. Lifting — 3×/week full body

You're already doing 2–3. The goal is consistency plus enough volume per muscle to actually drive adaptation.

**The dose.** Pelland et al. 2025 (Sports Medicine, Bayesian meta-regression, 67 studies, N=2,058) found hypertrophy follows a **square-root** curve — **+0.24% muscle size per additional weekly set** — while strength follows a **reciprocal** curve that plateaus much earlier (+0.21%/set). Minimum effective dose is around **4 fractional sets per muscle per week**; the useful range for someone with your goals is roughly **8–12 sets per muscle per week**, and the returns past that are real but small and cost time you don't have.

**Frequency.** At equated volume, 2× per muscle per week beats 1×. With 3 full-body sessions you hit everything 3×, which makes per-session volume low and recovery easy — the right shape for someone whose failure mode is not showing up.

**Proximity to failure.** You don't need to train to failure. Leaving 1–3 reps in reserve captures nearly all the hypertrophy stimulus at meaningfully lower fatigue cost, which matters when you're also running twice a week.

### The template

Three sessions, A/B/A then B/A/B the following week. ~45–55 minutes.

**Session A**
| Exercise | Sets × reps | RIR |
|---|---|---|
| Squat or leg press | 3 × 5–8 | 2 |
| Bench press or DB press | 3 × 6–10 | 2 |
| Row (barbell/cable/DB) | 3 × 8–12 | 2 |
| Romanian deadlift | 2 × 8–12 | 2–3 |
| Lateral raise | 2 × 12–15 | 1–2 |

**Session B**
| Exercise | Sets × reps | RIR |
|---|---|---|
| Deadlift or trap-bar DL | 2 × 4–6 | 2–3 |
| Overhead press | 3 × 6–10 | 2 |
| Pull-up or lat pulldown | 3 × 6–12 | 2 |
| Split squat or leg curl | 2 × 10–15 | 2 |
| Curl + triceps superset | 2 × 10–15 | 1–2 |

**Progression.** When you hit the top of the rep range on all sets at the prescribed RIR, add load next session (2.5–5 kg lower body, 1–2.5 kg upper). That's it. No periodisation needed at this training age.

**Two things you can stop worrying about.** Soreness is not a marker of adaptation — it tracks novelty and eccentric damage, not growth. And you do not need a scheduled deload at 3 sessions/week with RIR 2; if you feel beaten up, take an easy week when it happens.

## 4. Running — the Tue/Thu onramp

This is the part that "almost never" happens, so the design is built around adherence first and stimulus second.

**Why that's the right priority.** Fokkema et al. 2018 followed 774 novice runners: **29.5% had stopped by 26 weeks, and 48% of quitters cited injury**. Novice injury incidence is **17.8 per 1000 hours** vs 7.7 for established recreational runners (Videbæk et al. 2015), front-loaded into the first 8–13 weeks. **Injury prevention *is* the adherence strategy** — they're not competing goals.

And the strongest modifiable dropout predictor was *"uncertainty about continuing after the programme"* (**OR 2.06**). So decide now what happens in week 17, before week 1.

### Weeks 1–4 — deliberately, almost embarrassingly easy
- 5 min brisk walk → **6 × (1 min jog / 2 min walk)** → 5 min walk. ~28 min.
- **Cap week 1 at ~3 km total running.** This is the one number with a per-protocol RCT behind it: starting at 3 km/week vs 6 km/week cut injury risk by 31.2% in novices.
- Each week add 30 s to the jog interval (1:00 → 1:30 → 2:00 → 2:30), keep 6 reps.

### Weeks 5–8
- Progress toward **4 × 4 min jog / 2 min walk**.
- From week 6, Thursday becomes the harder day: **4 × 1 min at hard-but-controlled effort**, 2 min walk between.

### Weeks 9–16
- **Tuesday:** easy continuous 20–30 min. Conversational.
- **Thursday: 4 × 4 min at ~90–95% HRmax, 3 min active recovery.** This is Helgerud's protocol — **+7.2% VO2max in 8 weeks** at 3×/week in moderately trained men, and it was the only arm that moved VO2max when total work was matched against 45 min of steady running (which produced *no change*).

### Guardrails

- **Forget the 10% rule.** A 532-person RCT (Buist et al. 2008) tested a graded programme built on it: injury 20.8% vs 20.3%, **p = .90**. Null.
- **Use this instead:** never let a single run exceed **110% of your longest run in the previous 30 days**. Nielsen et al. 2024 (5,205 runners, 588,071 sessions) found injuries are *acute load-spike events*, not gradual accumulations — a 10–30% jump over your recent peak carried **+64% injury risk**, >100% carried **+128%**.
- **Two missed weeks → restart the previous 4-week block.** Detraining plus a load spike is the classic novice injury path.
- **Any pain that changes your gait ends the session.** One avoided injury is worth more than four weeks of progression.

### Missed a run? Stair snacks.

3 × 30 s all-out stair climbs, at least an hour apart. Yin et al. 2024 (RCT, N=42): that protocol at 3 days/week for 6 weeks — **~27 total minutes of work** — produced **+2.5 mL/kg/min VO2peak (+7%)** while a moderate continuous training arm gained nothing significant. This keeps the week alive at near-zero cost.

### Will running kill your gains? No.

Wilson et al. 2012 said yes and specifically blamed running. **Schumann et al. 2022 (43 studies, N=1,090) did not replicate it**: maximal strength SMD **−0.06 (ns)**, hypertrophy SMD **−0.01 (ns)**. Only *explosive* strength showed interference (−0.28), and mainly when both were done in the same session. Rule: **separate lifting and running by ≥3 hours, or put them on different days.** Never run hard legs before a leg session.

## 5. Social connection

Holt-Lunstad et al. 2010 (**148 studies, N=308,849**): stronger social relationships, **OR 1.50** for survival. And risk is *higher* in midlife than old age (adjusted OR 1.57 under 65 vs 1.14 over 75).

**Two honesty notes.** First, drop the "equivalent to 15 cigarettes a day" framing — it's a relative-risk comparison across incommensurable exposures, and the same paper found loneliness comparable to obesity, which nobody quotes. Second, and more important: Zhang et al. 2024 (UK Biobank, N=476,100, observational + MR) found that of 26 diseases, **20 showed non-causal associations** — loneliness looks like "a potential surrogate marker rather than a causal risk factor" for most physical disease. **What survives MR is the mental-health link**, and it survives strongly (depression OR 5.55).

So: do this for how it feels, not for the mortality table.

**What the evidence actually points at:** the mortality-predictive measure was *complex social integration* (**OR 1.91**) — network breadth and role diversity. Binary living-alone status was **null**. That's why the target is a **recurring group commitment** plus 2 substantive contacts, not a daily "did you talk to someone" checkbox. A standing weekly thing converts intention into a scheduled default and is the only item that builds breadth rather than one-to-one contact.

One cheap, well-evidenced nudge: Epley & Schroeder (2014) found people assigned to talk to a stranger on their commute had a better journey (**d = 0.56–0.63**) with no productivity cost — while separate samples predicted the *opposite*. You will systematically underestimate how good small social contact feels.

## 6. Nutrition — three things, not thirty

**Protein: ~1.6 g/kg/day.** Morton et al. 2018 (49 RCTs, N=1,863) — supplementation added **+0.30 kg FFM** and **+2.49 kg 1RM**, with the benefit plateauing around 1.6 g/kg. Note the honest magnitude: 0.30 kg is the *entire* pooled effect. Protein is a floor to clear, not a lever to obsess over.

**Adherence beats macros.** DIETFITS (Gardner et al. 2018, JAMA, N=609, 12 months) found **no significant difference** between healthy low-fat and healthy low-carb, and no interaction with genotype or insulin secretion. POUNDS Lost (N=811, 2 years) found the same across four macro splits. Whatever you'll actually stick to is the correct diet.

**Creatine monohydrate, 3–5 g/day.** The most consistently supported supplement in the category, cheap, and it doesn't need loading or cycling.

**What's not worth tracking:** meal timing. The TREAT trial (Lowe et al. 2020, JAMA Internal Medicine, N=116) found 16:8 produced **−0.94 kg vs −0.68 kg for controls, p = .63** — and the fasting group lost significantly **more lean mass** (p = .005). Time-restricted eating is a calorie-reduction tactic that works if it reduces your calories, and does nothing if it doesn't.

## 7. Parked — deliberately not in the system

This list exists because leaving things out silently looks like an oversight. Each of these is parked for a stated reason, and parking is reversible.

| Practice | Why it's parked |
|---|---|
| **Cold plunge after lifting** | ⚠️ **Actively counterproductive.** Roberts et al. 2015 (*J Physiol*): 12 weeks of training with post-session cold water immersion vs active recovery — **type II fibre CSA +17% in the non-cold arm only**, myonuclei +26% non-cold only, worse strength and mass gains with cold. Fyfe et al. 2019 replicated the fibre-level blunting (ES −1.37). If you ever do cold, keep it **≥4 hours away from lifting.** |
| **5am club** | No causal support. Genotype end-to-end difference in actual sleep timing: **25 minutes** (N=697,828). Consistency is the evidenced variable; earliness is aesthetics. |
| **Zone 2 as the priority** | At 2 sessions/week this is the wrong emphasis. 4 weeks of Zone 2 didn't raise citrate synthase or mitochondrial respiration in recreationally active men; work-matched, 45 min of steady running produced **no VO2max change** while 4×4 intervals produced +7.2%. The 80/20 rule comes from athletes training 10–20 h/week and the meta-analytic superiority (SMD 0.24) exists **only in highly trained athletes and only under 12 weeks**. |
| **Reading as brain health** | The flagship causal test kills it: Anderson et al. 2020 (MR, 17,008 AD cases) found education's protective effect **null once intelligence was controlled**. Bavishi et al. 2016's book-reading survival advantage shrank from 23 months to **4 months** on adjustment and was **fully mediated by cognition**. Read because you want to. Don't launder it as optimisation. |
| **Meditation** | Goyal et al. 2014 (47 RCTs with active controls): anxiety **0.22–0.38**, depression 0.23–0.30, and *"no evidence that meditation programs were better than any active treatment."* Cognitive effects vs active controls: **g = 0.07, null.** Keep it if you already do it. |
| **Gratitude journaling** | vs measurement-only control **d = .31**; vs an alternative activity **d = .17**; vs a psychologically active control **d = .03, ns** (Davis et al. 2016). It's roughly as good as any other structured daily reflection. |
| **16:8 fasting** | TREAT: p = .63 on weight, p = .005 on losing *more* lean mass. Zero human RCTs with mortality endpoints. |
| **Blue-blocker glasses** | Cochrane 2023, 17 RCTs: *"our findings do not support the prescription of blue-light filtering lenses to the general population."* |
| **Sauna** | HR 0.37 for sudden cardiac death sounds spectacular, but it's a single observational Finnish cohort where the exposure is near-perfectly confounded with being well enough to sit in an 80°C room five times a week. Pleasant. Not evidenced as an intervention. |
| **"Dopamine detox"** | The term's originator says it isn't about dopamine — it's CBT stimulus control with a catchy name. The behaviour is already in the system as binding blocks. |

## 8. What "top performer habits" are actually worth

A structural warning, because you asked for this specifically.

The genre is *In Search of Excellence* for individuals. When Rosenzweig went back to that book's 35 "excellent" companies with Compustat data: **30 declined, only 5 improved.** *Built to Last*'s 18 visionary companies: over half failed to match the market. *Good to Great*'s 11: Fannie Mae and Circuit City each lost **≥80%**, and Circuit City liquidated.

Those books had **auditable financials** and still got it 30-out-of-35 wrong. Morning-routine listicles have retrospective self-report from people who already know they succeeded, and no control group of equally disciplined people who didn't make it. There is no reason to think the personal version behaves better.

The move isn't to copy routines. It's to run the small number of things with actual dose-response data, and to check back in eight weeks.

## 9. Expectations, stated honestly

- **Habit formation:** median 66 days, range **18–254**, and roughly **91 days for exercise** specifically. Only 48% of Lally's participants formed a fittable habit curve at all. Anyone promising 21 days is selling something.
- **The month 4–7 cliff is real.** Among people who eventually disengage from tracking, 57–74% do it in months 4–7. Expect it. **33–46% of people who fall off come back** if there's something to come back to.
- **Running:** at 2 sessions/week with one interval day, a realistic 16-week expectation is **+3 to +6 mL/kg/min** VO2max.
- **Lifting:** at 8–12 sets/muscle/week and consistent attendance, visible change on a 12–16 week horizon, not a 4-week one.
- **And the one to be most careful about:** in the gym megastudy (*Nature* 2021, N=61,293, 54 expert-designed arms), expert forecasts of which interventions would work correlated with actual results at **r = 0.02** and were **9.1× too optimistic**. That includes this document. It's ranked by evidence quality, which is knowable — not by what will work for you, which isn't.

**The review date is 8 weeks out.** Not to grade yourself — to look at the CSV and ask which items are actually getting ticked, park the ones that aren't, and stop pretending the rest are happening.
